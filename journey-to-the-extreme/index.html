About the book

See what can happen when someone responds to the realization "the purpose with my life must be more than this..." This is His Story…

Horn of Africa, Upper Rift Valley—Most people have decided to get a haircut or a new pair of shoes on a whim. However, few people decide to change their whole life’s purpose, move to the Horn of Africa and tour the rest of the underdeveloped world in search of frontiers where no one has been before, simply because of a dream. Karl Hargestam did just that.

In his newest book release The Assignment: Journey to the Extreme, Hargestam bases the story on true events. Karl has delivered the message of Christ to more than 3.5 million people. Hargestam founded Joshua Campaign, a pioneer mission and evangelism movement that reaches hundreds of thousands of people each year who have not been introduced to the Gospel of Christ.

The Assignment: Journey to the Extreme follows fictional characters Cole and Monique as well as local tribal leaders and witch doctors, characters derived from actual encounters during mission expeditions. After a series of dreams and seemingly coincidental opportunities, Cole decides to make a mission trip! It becomes the beginning of a new life Adventure for their family. By connecting to the assignment God has for Cole, he experiences a new world of purpose and meaning opening before him.

“By following the created characters in their adventure through this book, I believe people will be challenged to search for their ‘assignment.’ There is no greater place to be! It is amazing can what happen if you respond to opportunities that cross your path,” says Hargestam.

What the book is about

The Assignment: Journey to the Extreme opens in the small town of St. Clair, Indiana, with Cole Tarkington, a high school history teacher, his wife Monique, who runs a travel agency, and their 10-year-old son Eric. Their lives are full of everyday pressure. Money is tight, the principal, Giles, has his doubts about Cole, and a struggling student named Randy needs more help than a classroom can give. And Cole keeps having the same mysterious dream of a river, a dream that seems to be trying to tell him something.

Far away in Ethiopia, a tribesman named Teguash sets out on a dangerous journey on behalf of his people, and a villager named Aberra has to decide whether to step forward and help him. In Indiana, Mulu, a young woman furthering her education, crosses paths with Randy and with Cole’s church. When Rev. Hasbro’s church is given the chance to sponsor a mission work and Cole is offered the chance to carry it out, Monique resists, and the decision tests their marriage and their faith.

Once Cole lands in Ethiopia, he meets doubting fellow missionaries, the priests of the Horro, and a man named Tamrat who opposes him at every turn. Based on real events from Karl Hargestam’s mission work, the novel follows ordinary people as each of them discovers an assignment and has to decide what it will cost.

Chapter One: Unexpected Turns

“Do you prefer ice coffee?” Monique asked, slipping her feet into her shoes.

She noticed that scuff marks on one heel had almost wiped out the original shade of blue. A piece of cheaply manufactured brass trimming, originally a shiny invitation to the buyer’s eye, now tarnished the image even further. A pair of new shoes weren’t in the budget, but Monique might have to fandangle a way to purchase them.

“You know I hate ice coffee. Why would you ask me that, Mo?” Cole feigned being insulted that his wife didn’t know him better, if for no other reason than to conceal the fact his mind was unfocused.

“Ice coffee’s little more than frozen caffeine. Why not just streamline an IV into your arm and skip the middleman? There’s no taste to it.”

Monique quickly ran a tube of chapstick across her thread-like lips. “Well, you’ve been staring at that cup for 10 minutes. It’ll be ice, if it isn’t already, by the time you get around to taking the first sip. I thought maybe you’d acquired new tastes.”

Her terseness was just enough to match his. Surprisingly, she regretted that truth, for as she watched her husband run his hand through his thinning hair she became aware of how sleep deprived he was.

Quietly sighing out of frustration, she scanned the kitchen counter until she spotted a metal globe keychain atop a recipe book.

She snatched up her keys and wondered to herself why she kept those recipe books. She hadn’t had time to actually cook a dish listed in them for several months—or had it been a year already? The holidays weren’t the last time she’d cracked those books open, was it? Had it been that long? Another sigh passed over her lips.

“Mom!” a blonde boy sporting black and blue Adidas shoes impatiently fidgeted in the doorway with his hands clinched around the straps of a backpack he was lumbering around on his back. Eric flashed a glare of anxiety, an expression Monique knew he’d inherited from his father.

“I’m going to be late!” His volume punctuated his point before he turned to dash for the car.

“Can’t keep a 10-year-old waiting,” Monique noted leaning over to kiss her husband on the forehead.

“Don’t you be late either,” her authoritative air always made her appear larger than her trim five-foot, five-inch frame.

“Don’t worry, Mo,” Cole assured her, “my first hour is study hall. I’m just the chaperone. I only play a teacher on my resume.”

Cole sat staring blankly into the blackness of his coffee as he listened to the garage door close behind Monique’s car backing out of the driveway.

The murky coffee stared back at him as though taunting him. He took a sip and nearly spit it back out. Mo had been right; ice cold.

Disgusted with himself, he didn’t even dump it into the sink. In fact, he didn’t bother with breakfast at all. Telling himself he could afford to lose a few pounds, even though a true diet was weeks away, he grabbed his backpack with his work papers jammed in it and a jean jacket hanging on a hook behind the garage door, before heading for the high school that was home to the St. Clair Rams.

The town of St. Clair, founded among the rolling corn and soybean farm fields of Indiana, couldn’t exactly be deemed ambitious by nature.

Looking out across the students in his study hall, Cole accepted the reality, accented by the number of books still lingering in their backpacks that his students were typical in their lack of aspiration while in contrast holding expectations of living out the American middle class dream.

One of the cheerleaders, makeup perfectly applied, was on top of her flirt game even this early in the morning. He watched her slowly reeling in one of the football players with a perfected facade of innocence. There was even a shallow laugh accompanying the performance.

A clique huddled in the back was cutting up over some escapade that had gone awry the prior night. Cole should have told them to quiet down and break out their books to at least give the pretense they cared about their grades. Instead, he pulled out a stack of history papers requiring grading by third period.

“No, they weren’t protesting the Iraq War at Kent State,” Cole heard himself mumble as he placed an exaggerated red mark on the paper.

He rubbed his eyes. He should’ve called in sick so he could’ve got some sleep. At the very least, he should’ve stolen the pot of coffee out of the teacher’s lounge.

A fluorescent light above him flickered intermittently, irritating the fatigue headache gaining steam inside his skull. The strobe light effect didn’t bother Randy Shipp, though, whose sleepy drool was landing on an old gum wrapper on his desk.

Presumably, the gum was still lodged in his mouth. Cole hoped the kid didn’t choke on it. At least he wasn’t snoring.

Cole wondered if it were booze or drugs Randy was sleeping off this morning. Then he chuckled when he spotted the long-haired kid was sporting a blue sock on his left foot and no sock at all on his right.

Most of the teachers wrote Randy off as a lost cause, but Cole liked him, although he acknowledged the other teachers were probably on target with their assessment. Man, it was going to be a long, long day.

*  *  *

“I totally understand, Mr. Haynesworth. If you decide to reschedule the trip in the future, you’ll call again, won’t you?”

Monique comprehended all too well why companies were postponing booking trips with her travel agency, and she didn’t have to stare long at the calculator to grasp the devastating impact to her bottom line. These days, everybody’s wallet was on life support. She was a good money manager so she’d survive, but mad money had grown scarce.

“Did they cancel?” Monique’s assistant asked, pretending there was suspense.

In her late twenties, Lydia had been working as Monique’s assistant for three years. She enjoyed the job, but was becoming increasingly concerned with the fall off in business.

“Oui,” speaking even a word in her native tongue boosted Monique’s self-confidence. A strand of her brunette hair flopped down into her big brown eyes. She swept it back.

“Is everything ok, Mrs. Tarkington?” Lydia asked.

Monique gathered herself with a deep breath, “Oui, bien sur. Tout va bien. Yes, yes, it’s fine.”

“And at home? I mean, things ok with Mr. Tarkington and little Eric? You know you can talk to me if they aren’t. After all, I think of us as friends first.”

Monique wished she could spill her fears. What a relief it’d be just to open up and let all her worries about Cole hang out there for someone else to share. At least then, if someone else saw what she saw, she wouldn’t feel like she was being paranoid about phantom problems.

It couldn’t be labeled a phantom, though, when she witnessed Cole crying out in his sleep, sweating as though he’d run a marathon, and waking up with his heart racing just as hard.

This—this—whatever it was plaguing Cole—had been going on for a week straight now. Every night was a repeat of the night before. The terrible part? Cole wasn’t talking. Whatever was disrupting his sleep was stuck inside his head, and he was determined to keep it locked up there. Being shut out from the depths of his mind made her heart a playground for fear.

Lydia pressed the issue harder as she laid a fax down on Monique’s desk, “You’d tell me if something wasn’t right, wouldn’t you?”

“Absolutely,” Monique inherited her ability to hide things close to her vest from her father, who’d had half a decade of incorporating the trait as a kid surviving the war back in his French village.

She wondered if her mother, whom she was named after, also possessed that ability. Monique’s mother had been fatally injured in an automobile accident days after her birth. Outside of her name and a few old photos, Monique had gleaned little knowledge of her mother. It’d always been too painful for her father to speak of her, and Monique learned at an early age not to bring up the topic.

Her assistant was one of the business cuts Monique was considering making if the business downturn persisted. Best not to get any closer than they already were. Monique thought maybe she would leave early this afternoon and buy some new shoes after all. Forget what the checkbook balance read.
